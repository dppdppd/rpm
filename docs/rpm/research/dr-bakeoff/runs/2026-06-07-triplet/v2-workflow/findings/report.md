# VOC expedition goals and the documents that set them, 1602–1700

**Research question.** What goals did the VOC's chambers, the Heren XVII (the central board of seventeen directors), the High Government at Batavia, and individual officers assign to Dutch East India Company expeditions between 1602 and 1700, and through which issuing documents were those goals conveyed? Three Dutch-primary sub-questions probe the founding charter, Coen's 1621 Banda dispatch, and Jan van Riebeeck's 1651 outbound register.

**Method note.** Every load-bearing claim below was checked against a fetched artifact window before being written, and each was run through a four-lens adversarial panel (provenance, cross-source, referent, alt-hypothesis). Where the panel killed a claim and a better-supported rival reading existed, the rival is asserted here and the original is recorded only in `../validation/refuted.md`. Confidence tags: **HIGH** = confirmed verbatim in a primary or authoritative source; **MEDIUM** = confirmed in substance but with a source-attribution or scope caveat; **LOW** = single weak source or inference.

A recurring caveat runs through this report: the most-cited "primary" Dutch texts online are not the 1602/1621/1651 manuscripts themselves but later transcriptions or modern renderings. The Dutch Wikisource charter is an 18th-century adaptation by Valentyn; the Nationaal Archief Banda page gives a modern Dutch *hertaling* of Coen's letter; the Daghregister text is the 1952 Bosman & Thom scholarly edition. The substance is sound, but the institutional and archival pin-points in the original question do not all hold, and those discrepancies are flagged inline.

## 1. The founding instrument: the 1602 charter (octrooi) and the board of seventeen

The VOC was created by a charter (*octrooi*) granted by the States General of the United Netherlands at The Hague on 20 March 1602. The charter is the foundational issuing document of the whole system: it set up the chambers, the central board, and the rule that the board's resolutions were to be executed by the chambers.

**Per-chamber allocation of the seventeen seats (sub-question 1).** Article II of the charter fixes the composition of the central board (the body that became known as the Heren XVII). Translating the Dutch directly: *"That, as often as it shall be necessary to hold a general Assembly or College out of the aforesaid chambers, it shall be held of 17 persons; therein from the chamber of Amsterdam shall appear 8, from Zeeland 4, from the Maaze 2, and likewise from Noord-Holland 2."* The allocation is therefore **Amsterdam 8, Zeeland 4, the Maze (Meuse: Rotterdam/Delft) 2, and Noord-Holland (West-Friesland: Hoorn/Enkhuizen) 2 — sixteen fixed seats.** [HIGH] (Dutch original, Article II: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>; independently corroborated verbatim at <https://www.vocsite.nl/geschiedenis/octrooi1602/>)

**The rule for the seventeenth seat (sub-question 1).** The same article continues: *"wel verstaande, dat de zeventiende persoon by beurten van die van Zeeland, Maaze ende Noordholland, zal werden in de Vergadering gebragt by de meeste stemmen"* — **"it being well understood that the seventeenth person shall be brought into the Assembly in turns by those of Zeeland, the Maaze, and Noord-Holland, by majority of votes."** The seventeenth (deciding) seat is therefore **not fixed to Amsterdam**; it rotates by turn among the three non-Amsterdam chamber-groups (Zeeland, the Maze, Noord-Holland), and the holder for each turn is chosen by majority vote of those three. This deliberately kept Amsterdam — which held eight of the sixteen fixed seats and supplied half of all capital — short of an outright majority on the board. [HIGH] (Dutch original, Article II: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>; corroborated at <https://www.vocsite.nl/geschiedenis/octrooi1602/>)

The exact rotation phrase in the surviving text is **"by beurten"** (by turns). The widely repeated paraphrase "bij toerbeurte" does not appear in the source. A further provenance caveat: the Dutch Wikisource page is explicitly an 18th-century Dutch rendering taken from François Valentyn's *Oud en Nieuw Oost-Indiën* (deel 1, pp. 186–191), and the page's own closing note states that the clause numbering and modernised wording "did not appear in the original 1602 manuscript." The seat numbers and the rotation rule are faithful to the original; the wording is Valentyn's, not the 1602 chancery's. [HIGH] (page metadata and editorial note: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>). The original 1602 manuscript itself is held by the Nationaal Archief in VOC archive 1.04.02 and is presented in the institution's teaching resource (Bronnenbox). [HIGH] (<https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/octrooi-voor-de-voc-1602>)

**Goals set by the charter.** The charter assigned the company both commercial and quasi-sovereign objectives. Article III directs the College to "resolve when to equip, with how many ships, where to send them, and other matters concerning trade," and orders that "the resolutions of the aforesaid College shall be executed (*geeffectueert*) and put into effect by the chambers of Amsterdam, Zeeland, Maaze, and Noord-Holland" — establishing the basic division of authority for the next two centuries: **the board sets policy, the chambers execute it.** [HIGH] (Article III: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>) Article IV fixes the presiding chamber and meeting place: "the first 6 years in Amsterdam, and the 2 years after that in Zeeland," rotating thereafter — the documented basis for the later "Amsterdam six years, Middelburg two years" cycle. [HIGH] (Article IV: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>) Article XXXV grants the company, east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan, the power "to make alliances and contracts in the name of the States General with princes and potentates, to build fortresses, and to appoint governors, men of war, and officers of justice" — the charter clause that turned a trading venture into a territorial and military power, with the explicit goals of conquest, fort-building, and treaty-making. [HIGH] (Article XXXV: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>) Article XLV further obliges returning admirals or fleet commanders to render a written report (*relaas*) of their voyage — the charter's own embryonic accountability requirement on expeditions. [HIGH] (Article XLV: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>)

## 2. How the Heren XVII set expedition goals: the meeting cycle and its documents

The Heren XVII was the company's primary decision-making body, drawn from the six chambers. Its strategic objectives for outbound fleets were set on a regular annual rhythm and recorded in formal documents.

**The two-meeting rhythm.** The directors "would ideally meet twice a year." The **long autumn meeting** organised the autumn auction and "decided upon the number of ships, soldiers and armament to be sent off in the next year, and responded to any news that required immediate action." The shorter **spring meeting** "discussed the Asian situation in more detail, did a lot of reckoning and accounting, decided upon the protection measures for the return fleets, and usually also resolved upon the number of ships to be built." [HIGH] (<https://vocwarfare.net/thesis/3/decision-making>)

**The autumn meeting and the Generale Eis.** At the autumn meeting, before the Christmas fleet sailed, the directors handled the *Eisen van Retouren* (setting the volume of trade goods to be returned from Batavia for the following year's auction) and the **Generale Eis** — "basically the 'shopping list' of the government in Batavia," containing "requests for weapons, trade goods and provisions, ranging from bacon to rooftiles, from currency to cannonballs." The Generale Eis is the principal mechanism by which Batavia's material requirements were turned into per-chamber outfitting obligations for the next outbound fleet. [HIGH] (<https://vocwarfare.net/thesis/3/decision-making>)

**The spring meeting as the accountability mechanism.** It was specifically the **spring** meeting (not the autumn meeting) that did the reckoning: "the meeting of the Gentlemen XVII attempted to strengthen its grip on the various Chambers, and made resolution after resolution obliging the Chambers to account for the amount of artillery they had in stock, the number of people and material that had been sent off in the past year, and more such things." The same spring resolutions "increasingly specified the way in which ships had to be built." Resolutions thus functioned both as directives (autumn: what to send) and as an accountability instrument (spring: account for what was sent). [HIGH] (<https://vocwarfare.net/thesis/3/decision-making>)

> Correction to a common framing. It is tempting to attribute the whole cycle — directives *and* artillery-accountability — to the autumn meeting. The source places the accountability resolutions explicitly in the spring meeting paragraph. The autumn/spring functions are distinct and should not be merged. (See `../validation/refuted.md`, claim R-2.)

**The document classes of the Heren XVII archive.** The records of the Heren XVII and the Amsterdam chamber, held at the Nationaal Archief in VOC archive 1.04.02 (*Inventaris van het archief van de Verenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC), 1602–1795*), consist mainly of **Resoluties** (resolutions/proceedings), reports of the **Haags Besogne** (a standing correspondence sub-committee), letters to and documents received from the colonies, and — after 1700 — accounting documents, muster rolls, and ship's pay-books. [HIGH] (collection title and scope: <https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/onderzoeken/archief/1.04.02>) The archive also preserves secret resolutions: inventory item 215 is titled *"Secrete resoluties van de Heren XVII betreffende de uitzending van commissarissen-generaal naar Indië. Met bijlagen"* (1790–1791), confirming that politically sensitive instructions were a recognised sub-category of the board's output. [HIGH] (<https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/onderzoeken/archief/1.04.02>)

**Division of issuing responsibility.** The formal split, set in the charter and operative throughout the century, was: the Heren XVII took the binding policy decisions through resolutions; the individual chambers were the executors who built ships, recruited crews, and loaded goods. The presiding chamber rotated — Amsterdam for six consecutive years, then Middelburg (Zeeland) for two. [HIGH] (rotation in charter Article IV: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>; policy/execution split in charter Article III: <https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC>)

## 3. Coen's 1621 Banda dispatch: an expedition report back to the Heren XVII

Where the charter and resolutions flow *outward* (board → fleet), the Banda dispatch shows the *upward* reporting channel: a governor-general reporting the results of a military expedition to the board that authorised it.

**Archival location (sub-question 2).** Jan Pieterszoon Coen's letter reporting the Banda campaign to the Heren XVII, dated **6 May 1621**, is held at the Nationaal Archief in **VOC archive 1.04.02, inventarisnummer 1073.** This is confirmed by the Nationaal Archief's own Banda teaching page, which captions a document image: *"In een brief aan de Heren XVII doet Coen verslag van de gebeurtenissen op Banda 6 mei 1621 — NA 1.04.02 Archief Verenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC), inv.nr. 1073,"* and whose source line reads *"Brief Coen: 1.04.02 inventarisnummer 1073."* [HIGH] (<https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/hoe-ging-het-verder-op-banda>) The standard published transcription of Coen's correspondence is H.T. Colenbrander's *Jan Pietersz. Coen: bescheiden omtrent zijn bedrijf in Indië*, of which Volume 1 (1919) is freely available at the Internet Archive. [HIGH] (<https://archive.org/details/janpieterszcoenb01coen>)

**(a) Coen's own losses in the hill engagement, and the named officer lost (sub-question 2).** In the modern Dutch rendering (*hertaling*) of the dispatch published by the Nationaal Archief, Coen describes the engagement along the mountain ridge of Lonthor: after attacking the enemy's hill fortifications, his force was closely pursued on the way back along the narrow ridge. He reports: *"In deze ontmoeting kregen wij 35 gewonden en 9 doden, waaronder kapitein De Ros met zijn vaandeldrager."* That is, in that hill engagement **Coen's own force had 35 wounded and 9 killed, among them Captain De Ros together with his standard-bearer (vaandeldrager / ensign).** [HIGH] (<https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/hoe-ging-het-verder-op-banda>)

> Note on a separate set of figures. A different and lower casualty count — 6 killed and 27 wounded — belongs to the earlier coastal assault on Lonthor of 11–12 March 1621, not to the hill engagement, and is recorded in secondary narratives. [MEDIUM] (<https://jpcoen.wordpress.com/j-p-coen-en-de-banda-eilanden-in-1621-verslag-van-de-bezetting/>) Sub-question 2 asks specifically about the hill engagement, where the figures are 9 killed and 35 wounded.

**(b) What the ~1,200 figure refers to (sub-question 2).** Coen writes: *"In totaal hebben wij omtrent 1200 zielen gekregen … T'Postpaert gaat mee om de gevangenen te helpen bewaren en tot Jakarta voor de handel gebruikt te worden"* — **"in total we have obtained about 1,200 souls … the *Postpaert* goes along to help guard the prisoners and to be used at Jakarta for trade."** The ~1,200 figure therefore refers to **Bandanese taken captive in the conquest and shipped to Batavia (Jakarta) as prisoners — to be used there as enslaved trade labour.** It is *not* Coen's own force strength, and it is *not* a Dutch casualty figure. [HIGH] (<https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/hoe-ging-het-verder-op-banda>) The immediate context confirms the referent: the prisoners were sent on the ship *Dragon* to Jakarta, and a separate group of 45 Orang Kaya (Bandanese headmen) was pulled back off the *Dragon* and arrested.

> Caution on an editorial gloss. The Nationaal Archief page inserts a parenthetical editor's note "(red.: zijn circa 1200 man overleden)" — "(ed.: about 1,200 men died)." This gloss contradicts Coen's own surrounding sentences, which describe the 1,200 as living prisoners being guarded and shipped to Jakarta for trade. The defensible reading from the dispatch text itself is **captives**, not deaths. (See `../validation/refuted.md`, claim R-3.)

This dispatch is itself a worked example of the *upward* reporting instrument: a field commander reporting an expedition's military results, casualties, captives, and cargo (cloves, nutmeg, mace shipped on the *Schiedam*) directly to the Heren XVII.

## 4. Van Riebeeck's 1651 Daghregister: a fleet's outbound register

The register Jan van Riebeeck kept on the 1651 voyage to found the Cape refreshment station shows the *journal* genre — the running log a commander kept on an outbound expedition, here opening with a formal heading that names the actors, ships, and dispatching authority.

**Source caveat first.** Sub-question 3 pins the heading to "Nationaal Archief 1.04.02, inventarisnummer 1188, folio 189." The authoritative scholarly edition — Bosman & Thom's 1952 *Daghregister* — gives the manuscript's classification differently: its own footnote locates the original in the **Koloniaal Archief, no. 3966, folio 26**, held at the Rijksarchief in The Hague (renamed Nationaal Archief in 2002). [HIGH] (<https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>) The 1.04.02/1188/folio 189 reference appears in a Nationaal Archief teaching resource for a Van Riebeeck *letter*, a different document. I therefore answer sub-question 3 from the register heading as published in the Bosman & Thom edition (the closest openly accessible transcription of the manuscript heading), not from the disputed pin.

The opening register heading reads: *"Daghregister, gehouden bij den oppercoopman Jan Anthonisz. van Riebeeck, vertrocken per de schepen Drommedaris, Reijger ende Goede Hoope voor opperhooft naer Cabo de bo[a] Esperance, in dienst van de Generale Vereenighde Nederlantsche G'octroyeerde Oostindische Comp. uijt Texel, van de Camer Amsterdam."* [HIGH] (<https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>)

From that heading:

**(a) The three ships.** **Drommedaris, Reijger, and Goede Hoope** (the *Goede Hoop*). The Drommedaris was the flagship and the largest of the three. [HIGH] (<https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>)

**(b) His rank/role.** The heading names him **"oppercoopman"** (upper-merchant / chief merchant) and gives his shipboard command role as **"voor opperhooft"** — "as chief / head" of the expedition. He is *not* styled "governor" or "commandeur" in the heading. An editorial footnote on the same page clarifies the nuance: "oppercoopman" was the **salary grade** assigned to him on appointment; his formal appointment title was **Kommandeur** of the Cape station, which carried the rank "Koopman en Opperhoofd." [HIGH] (heading and footnote (a): <https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>) The published edition title — *Daghregister gehouden by den oppercoopman Jan Anthonisz. van Riebeeck*, ed. D.B. Bosman & H.B. Thom, Vol. I covering 1651–1655 (1952) — preserves "oppercoopman" exactly. [HIGH] (<https://antiquarianauctions.com/lots/daghregister-gehouden-by-den-oppercoopman-jan-anthonisz-van-riebeeck-3-vols-1>)

**(c) The port and chamber he sailed under.** He sailed **"uijt Texel, van de Camer Amsterdam"** — **out of Texel, under the Chamber of Amsterdam.** These are two distinct geographies: Texel was the physical anchorage and sailing point (the harbour from which Amsterdam-chamber ships customarily departed for the East Indies, as the same edition's footnote explains), while the **Amsterdam chamber** was the dispatching administrative authority. [HIGH] (heading and footnote (c): <https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>) The Daghregister's dated entries confirm the dual geography in motion: Van Riebeeck left the city of Amsterdam on 14 December 1651, boarded the Drommedaris at the Balgh near Texel on 16 December, and the fleet sailed from Texel on 24 December 1651. [HIGH] (<https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/rieb001dagh01_01/rieb001dagh01_01_0004.php>; ship build-date and 24 Dec departure also at <https://www.vocsite.nl/schepen/11538/>)

> Note on captains. The question's heading-derived answer concerns ships, rank, and port/chamber only. Skipper names (Davit Coninck for the Drommedaris, Jan Hooch[saet] for the Reijger, Symen Turver for the Goede Hoop) are not part of the register heading; they appear in the Council-of-Policy resolutions, a distinct archival series, and in the body of the diary — so they are not asserted here as part of the heading answer.

## 5. The other half of the system: Batavia as a commanding authority

By the period 1602–1700 expedition goals were not set in Europe alone. The High Government in Asia issued its own commands.

**The High Government.** The VOC's Supreme Government in Asia — the Governor-General and the Council of the Indies (Raad van Indië) — was established in 1609–1610 and sat in Batavia Castle, exercising substantial de facto command authority over expeditions in Asian waters given the roughly nine-month communication lag with Europe. [MEDIUM] (establishment and seat broadly corroborated; the specific "nine ordinary members from 1617" figure is contested — several Dutch sources give the Council's ordinary membership as usually six — so the exact council size is not asserted here. See `../validation/refuted.md`, claim R-7.)

**Batavia's outgoing instruments.** Batavia maintained its own outgoing correspondence series, the **Bataviaasch Uitgaand Briefboek (BUB), 1621–1792**, containing letters from VOC headquarters in Batavia to its factories across Asia and to the Heren XVII; this series set out company policy in the East — how trade should be conducted, where goods should be procured, where private traders could operate, and the rules on waging war and dealing with local rulers. [HIGH] (Dijk 2003: <https://os.pennds.org/archaeobib_filestore/pdf_articles/SOAS/2003_1_1_Dijk.pdf>; date range independently confirmed via the University of Tokyo SHIPS catalogue: <https://wwwap.hi.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ships/w45/search>) The General Resolutions of Batavia Castle (1613–1810) — the authorised, holograph-signed final decisions of the Supreme Government — survive in 331 volumes / 211,000+ pages, fully digitised, and contain not just bare decisions but deliberations, motivations, and inserted letters and documents. [HIGH] (ANRI Sejarah Nusantara: <https://sejarah-nusantara.anri.go.id/generalresolutions/>; digitisation corroborated by the Corts Foundation)

**The upward annual letter.** The principal upward-reporting instrument from Batavia to Europe was the **Generale Missiven van Gouverneurs-Generaal en Raden aan Heren XVII** — annual general letters from Batavia summarising all Asian operations (expedition results, commanders' activities, markets, relations with rulers, treaties). The published series runs across multiple volumes in the Rijks Geschiedkundige Publicatiën, beginning in 1610. [MEDIUM] (series and function: <https://resources.huygens.knaw.nl/vocgeneralemissiven>. The "thirteen volumes / 1610–1761" framing is now outdated — a fourteenth volume covering 1761–1767 exists — so the volume count is not asserted as fixed. See `../validation/refuted.md`, claim R-8.)

**Monopoly enforcement: the Hongi expeditions.** For spice-monopoly enforcement, Batavia dispatched **Hongi expeditions (*hongi-tochten*)** — periodic armed patrols of *kora-kora* war canoes, ordered by the Governor-General and Council, to extirpate unauthorised clove trees, punish smugglers, and enforce fixed-quota deliveries. The word *hongi* derives from Ternate (meaning "armada / fleet"). The key mid-century enforcement campaign was waged by Arnold de Vlamingh van Oudshoorn in the Moluccas between 1651 and 1656. [HIGH for mechanics, etymology, dates] (<https://www.atlasofmutualheritage.nl/page/2425/portrait-of-arnold-de-vlamingh-van-oudshoorn>) His operative title in that campaign was **superintendent of the eastern districts** (with "governor of Amboina" as the related gubernatorial role) — not "Governor of the Moluccas." [HIGH] (Atlas of Mutual Heritage portrait inscription; Livinus Bor, *Amboinse Oorlogen*. See `../validation/refuted.md`, claim R-9.)

## 6. Synthesis: the document types that assigned expedition goals

Pulling the threads together, expedition goals over 1602–1700 were assigned through a layered set of instruments:

- **The charter (octrooi, 1602)** — the foundational grant. It set the company's standing goals (trade monopoly east of the Cape, the power to build forts, appoint governors, wage war, and make treaties) and the constitutional machinery (chambers, the seventeen-seat board, the policy-sets / chambers-execute split). [HIGH]

- **Resoluties of the Heren XVII** — the recurring, binding strategic decisions taken twice yearly: the autumn meeting set the numbers of ships, soldiers, and armament for the coming fleet; the spring meeting did the reckoning, accountability, and shipbuilding specifications. [HIGH]

- **The Generale Eis** — Batavia's annual "shopping list," turned by the autumn meeting into per-chamber outfitting obligations. [HIGH]

- **Chamber-level execution** — the chambers built, crewed, and loaded the ships the board's resolutions called for, the presiding chamber rotating Amsterdam-six / Middelburg-two. [HIGH]

- **Batavia's instruments** — the General Resolutions of Batavia Castle, the Bataviaasch Uitgaand Briefboek, and individual orders to commanders (including the Hongi patrols), through which the High Government set operational and enforcement objectives in Asia without waiting on Europe. [HIGH/MEDIUM]

- **Upward reporting** — the Generale Missiven (Batavia → Heren XVII) and field dispatches like Coen's 1621 Banda letter, which reported expedition results, casualties, captives, and cargo back up the chain. [HIGH]

The three Dutch-primary probes anchor this picture at its endpoints: the 1602 charter article shows the board being *constituted* (16 fixed seats plus a rotating seventeenth chosen by majority of Zeeland/Maze/Noord-Holland); Coen's 1621 dispatch shows a commander *reporting* a conquest (9 dead and 35 wounded with Captain De Ros lost in the hill fight; ~1,200 Bandanese taken captive to Batavia); and Van Riebeeck's 1651 register shows an expedition *setting out* (the Drommedaris, Reijger, and Goede Hoope, under oppercoopman Van Riebeeck as opperhooft, sailing from Texel under the Chamber of Amsterdam).
